Tracking investments is less about refreshing prices every hour and more about knowing what you own, why you own it, and whether the plan still fits your life. A calm review rhythm beats a dashboard that makes you anxious.

## What “tracking” should actually answer

A useful review answers a few plain questions:

- Are balances and holdings still where you think they are (broker, platform, super, bank)?
- Have fees, interest, or distributions changed in a way that matters?
- Has your goal, time horizon, or cash need changed?
- Are you concentrated in one employer, one company, or one theme without meaning to be?

If you cannot answer those from statements and a short note, you are not tracking—you are guessing. ClearMoney’s [Net Worth](/tools/net-worth) tool can help you see the household picture without turning every share price into a mood swing.

## Build a holdings map once

Spend one sitting listing every account: bank savings, term deposits, share brokerage, managed funds, ETFs, super, and anything “alternative.” Note the provider, account number (or HIN), and where login MFA lives. Store that list somewhere your partner or executor could find—not inside a password manager note that only you can unlock.

People lose money in quiet ways when an old broker holds orphan shares, a dividend reinvestment plan compounds into a stock they no longer want, or a workplace ESS grant sits untracked beside their main portfolio. Mapping stops those surprises.

## Choose a review cadence that matches the money

Everyday cash and high-interest savings deserve frequent glances. Long-term diversified portfolios usually need less theatre: a quarterly look at contributions and fees, and an annual deeper pass for tax documents, beneficiaries, and whether your [investing plan](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/develop-an-investing-plan) still matches reality.

Daily price watching rarely improves returns. It often tempts you to sell after headlines or buy after a tip. If a chart spikes your heart rate, lengthen the interval and shrink the screen time.

## Performance without self-deception

Compare like with like. A global share ETF and a cash account will diverge for years; that is not a “failure” of either product. Measure against the job you gave the money: emergency buffer, five-year house deposit, or decades-long growth sleeve.

Look at total return including distributions, not only price. Note fees in dollars once a year so percentage management costs feel real. Past performance still does not promise the future—treat glossy year charts as marketing context, not a forecast.

When something underperforms a peer for years, ask whether the mandate changed, fees rose, or you simply bought a higher-risk sleeve and then expected bond-like calm. Rebalancing back toward your target mix is often more useful than chasing last year’s winner. See [diversification](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/diversification).

## Paperwork that saves future-you

Keep PDFs of contract notes, annual statements, dividend summaries, and capital gains reports. Australian tax rules for investing can be fiddly; incomplete records make lodgement slower and more error-prone. Pair tracking with a light [investing and tax](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/investing-and-tax) habit: export CSVs after year-end, do not wait until July panic.

Also record major life changes on the same calendar: new dependants, a house purchase, job change, or approaching retirement. Those events should trigger a holdings review even if markets look quiet.

## Spotting noise, scams, and fake “portfolio managers”

Cold calls offering to “optimise” your holdings, strangers asking for remote access, or apps that need seed phrases are not tracking tools—they are threat patterns. Verify any firm yourself and read [check before you invest](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/check-before-you-invest). If money already moved under pressure, use [/urgent](/urgent) and [what to do if you've been scammed](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/what-to-do-if-youve-been-scammed).

Legitimate tracking never requires secrecy from your bank or payment in crypto gift cards.

## When simplicity beats sophistication

If you hold three low-cost diversified products and contribute automatically, your “system” might be a calendar reminder plus annual statements. That is enough. Spreadsheets help when you have many accounts; they hurt when updating them replaces sleeping or earning.

Obsessive tracking is a cost: time, stress, and impulsive trades. Aim for enough visibility to stay honest—and not so much that markets run your week.

## What to do next

- List every investment account in one note, then reconcile it against the latest statements this week.
- Set a recurring review (for example quarterly) and stick to goals, fees, and cash needs—not daily prices.
- Use [Net Worth](/tools/net-worth) to see the household view beside your [investing plan](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/develop-an-investing-plan).
- Export tax-related records after financial year end; see [investing and tax](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/investing-and-tax).
- Treat unsolicited “portfolio help” as a scam risk until proven otherwise via [protect yourself from scams](/topics/scams-safety/online-safety/protect-yourself-from-scams).
