Diversification is the practice of not letting one company, sector, country, or tip decide your financial future. It will not eliminate losses. It reduces the chance that a single failure wipes out years of saving.

::: tip
Ten similar stocks is concentration wearing a diversification costume—check industries and regions, not just ticker count.
:::

## What spreading risk actually means

Owning ten mining stocks is not broad diversification—it is ten versions of a similar bet. Real spread usually means:

- **Across companies** — many issuers, not a favourite few
- **Across industries** — banks, healthcare, tech, industrials, and so on
- **Across countries or regions** — Australian and international exposure
- **Across asset types** — for example shares plus some defensive holdings if your plan needs them

A low-cost broad share ETF or diversified managed fund is often how everyday investors get hundreds of holdings without picking them one by one. That is the opposite of collecting tips from chat groups.

## Concentration you may already have

Many Australians are more concentrated than they realise:

- **Employer shares or options** — job and wealth tied to one company ([employee share schemes](/topics/investing-planning/shares/employee-share-schemes))
- **Investment property** — a large slice of net worth in one address and one city’s economy
- **Industry bias** — working in resources or banking while also holding those sectors heavily in a DIY portfolio
- **Home country bias** — almost everything Australian, so local recessions hit wages and investments together

Diversification is not only “buy more things.” Sometimes it means refusing to add more of what already dominates your life.

## How much diversification is enough?

There is no magic number of stocks. Practically:

- A handful of shares leaves you exposed to company-specific news, scandals, and earnings misses.
- Broad index-style funds already embed diversification for the market they track.
- Adding exotic products “for diversification” can increase complexity and fees without improving outcomes—especially if those products are illiquid or hard to value ([private credit](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/what-is-private-credit)).

Diversification across genuinely different risks matters more than collecting labels. Two products that move together in a crisis do not diversify each other, even if their names sound different.

## Rebalancing without turning it into a hobby

Over time, winners grow into a larger share of the portfolio. That quietly increases concentration.

A simple approach: set target ranges in your [investing plan](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/develop-an-investing-plan) (for example “mostly diversified growth funds, some cash buffer”), review a couple of times a year, and nudge contributions or rebalance toward targets—not toward headlines. Daily checking rarely improves diversification; it mostly feeds anxiety ([track your investments](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/track-your-investments)).

If tax on selling is a concern, prefer directing new contributions to underweight areas before realising large gains. Keep records either way ([investing and tax](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/investing-and-tax)).

## Correlation shows up in crises

Assets that seem different in calm years can fall together when liquidity dries up. That does not make diversification useless—it means you still need cash buffers and time. Owning five products that all track similar Australian equities is branding diversity, not risk diversity.

Review overlap annually: fund holdings, employer stock, and property exposure on one page.

## International shares and currency

Holding global companies diversifies earnings sources. Currency moves add another swing—sometimes helpful, sometimes not. Currency-hedged and unhedged funds behave differently when the Australian dollar jumps. Pick deliberately for the role in your plan rather than by accident of which ETF was on sale in an ad.

## What to do next

- List your biggest concentrations: employer equity, property, single shares, or one sector.
- Prefer broad funds or ETFs for long-term growth money unless you have a documented reason not to.
- Write simple target ranges in your plan so “diversified” is measurable, not a vibe.
- Avoid stacking similar bets and calling it diversification—read [choose your investments](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/choose-your-investments).
- Review concentration at your next scheduled portfolio check, not after every market swing.
