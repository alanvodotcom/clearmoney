Employee share schemes (ESS) give staff a stake in the company through shares, rights, options, or similar interests—often with vesting conditions. They can align incentives and build wealth when the company does well. They also stack **job risk and investment risk** in one place: if the firm struggles, your income and your equity can fall together. This is general information, not tax or financial advice.

## Know what you were actually granted

Plan rules differ. Clarify:

- grant type (shares, options, performance rights, matching plans)
- vesting dates and performance hurdles
- forfeiture on resignation or dismissal
- disposal restrictions and blackout periods
- what happens on takeovers or delisting

Keep the plan booklet, grant letters, and annual ESS statements in one folder. HR portals change; PDFs endure.

## Concentration is the quiet hazard

A large unvested or vested position in your employer can dwarf the rest of your portfolio. That concentration feels loyal; financially it is fragile. Diversification principles still apply ([diversification](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/diversification)). Many people set a personal cap—once vested shares exceed a threshold, they systematically sell toward a diversified mix when windows allow.

Selling can have tax consequences and may be constrained by policy. Ignoring concentration because “I believe in the company” is still a risk choice.

## Tax timing—get personal guidance when stakes are large

Australian ESS tax rules depend on the scheme design, whether deferred taxing points apply, and your circumstances. Taxing points can arrive when you cannot easily sell, creating a cash-flow squeeze. Payroll may withhold amounts; you remain responsible for correct lodgement.

When grants are material, speak with a registered tax agent who understands ESS. Seminar generalities and Slack-thread folklore are not substitutes. ClearMoney does not provide tax advice—only a prompt to plan cash for tax events before they hit.

## Trading windows, insider rules, and clean habits

Listed employers usually restrict when you can trade. Breaking blackouts or using inside information is illegal and career-ending—see [insider trading](/topics/investing-planning/warnings/insider-trading). Use only approved channels to exercise or sell. Keep personal records of trades for your own files even when the company reports some events.

Unsolicited “advisers” offering to unlock employee stock early via offshore structures are a scam pattern. Verify anyone touching your equity ([check before you invest](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/check-before-you-invest)).

## Startups, pre-IPO equity, and paper wealth

Unlisted equity can look life-changing on a spreadsheet and remain unsaleable for years—or forever. Preference stacks, dilution, and failure rates are real. Treat unvested startup paper as a hopeful bonus, not as house-deposit arithmetic. If a listing arrives, IPO dynamics bring fresh volatility ([what is an IPO?](/topics/investing-planning/shares/shares-what-is-an-ipo)).

## Fit ESS into the wider plan

Salary, emergency savings, super, and diversified investments should not be neglected because equity “might” boom. An [investing plan](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/develop-an-investing-plan) that assumes modest ESS outcomes is more robust than one that requires a perfect vesting cliff. [Track](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/track-your-investments) vested value alongside other holdings so you see concentration in dollars, not vibes.

If you need personal financial advice about diversification or cash-flow around vesting, use an authorised adviser ([choosing a financial adviser](/topics/investing-planning/advice/choosing-a-financial-adviser)).

## Leaving the company cleanly

Resignation, redundancy, or dismissal can accelerate vesting, trigger forfeiture, or open short exercise windows. Read leaver clauses before you announce anything. Set calendar reminders for option expiry dates—expired options are a painful own-goal.

Update your personal holdings map when you leave so former-employer stock does not become a forgotten concentration sitting in an old portal you no longer check.

## What to do next

- File plan rules and grant letters; diary vesting and blackout dates.
- Measure employer equity as a percentage of your net investable assets—including unvested estimates separately.
- Plan cash for potential tax events; consult a registered tax agent when amounts are significant.
- When allowed, consider diversifying vested concentration into broader assets.
- Ignore strangers offering to “monetise” ESS early; verify via [check before you invest](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/check-before-you-invest).
