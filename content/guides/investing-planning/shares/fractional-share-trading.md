Fractional share trading lets you buy a slice of a share—or sometimes an ETF—when a full unit costs more than you want to spend in one go. Brokers pitch access and flexibility. Used carefully, fractions can help you diversify with small amounts. Used carelessly, they become a reason to trade constantly and ignore whole-share costs and rights.

## What you actually receive

A fractional purchase is usually a claim on a portion of a share held through your broker’s structure—not always the same as holding a whole CHESS-sponsored share in your name. Voting rights, dividend handling, corporate actions, and how you move holdings between brokers can differ from whole-share ownership.

Read the product or client agreement: are you a beneficial owner of a slice, and how are dividends credited? If the answer is vague, pause and compare with ordinary whole-share trading ([how to buy and sell shares](/topics/investing-planning/shares/how-to-buy-and-sell-shares)).

## When fractions earn their place

Fractions help when:

- a single share price is high relative to your planned contribution
- you want a small, scheduled buy without waiting to save a full unit
- you are building a diversified ETF position in small steps

They do **not** make a speculative tip safer. Owning 0.03 of a hot ticker is still a concentrated bet ([choosing shares to buy](/topics/investing-planning/shares/choosing-shares-to-buy)).

For many people, buying a broad ETF with whole or fractional units beats collecting tiny slices of many single stocks ([diversification](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/diversification)).

## Costs hide in the convenience

Watch for:

- wider spreads on fractional orders
- FX conversion if the stock is overseas
- brokerage or platform fees on every micro-trade
- custody or inactivity charges

Ten tiny buys can cost more than one deliberate purchase of a similar diversified product. Add annual costs in dollars and compare to a low-turnover approach on a reputable [investment platform](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/investment-platforms).

## Order types and partial fills

Fractional interfaces sometimes simplify order types. Confirm whether you are paying a market-like price, whether limits apply, and what happens if liquidity is thin. Wrong ticker + tiny size still creates a real position you must track for tax and statements.

Keep contract notes. Fractions do not exempt you from record-keeping.

## Rights, dividends, and leaving the broker

Ask before you need the answer:

- How are dividends paid on partial holdings?
- Can you transfer fractions to another broker, or must you sell first?
- What happens in a share split, takeover, or delisting?

Exit friction matters if you chose a broker mainly for a fractional feature you no longer use.

## Micro-app crossover risk

Fractional tiles often live beside round-ups and “discover” feeds in the same apps that market [micro investing](/topics/investing-planning/how-to-invest/micro-investing). The combination nudges frequent trading. Set a contribution schedule and mute discovery notifications if they turn investing into entertainment.

## Scam and clone caution

Licensed Australian brokers offering fractions are not the same as offshore apps advertising “own Tesla with $5” via crypto deposits. Verify credentials yourself ([check before you invest](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/check-before-you-invest)) and type URLs manually ([scam websites](/topics/scams-safety/online-safety/how-to-spot-a-scam-website)). Blocked withdrawals and remote-access “support” are scam patterns—[/urgent](/urgent) and [report an investment scam](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/report-an-investment-scam) if funds are stuck.

## A practical rule of thumb

Use fractions to **fund a plan** you already wrote. Do not use fractions to **justify** owning more single names than you can follow. If whole shares of a diversified ETF fit your contribution size, you may not need fractions at all.

## What to do next

- Read how your broker holds fractional interests, pays dividends, and handles transfers.
- Prefer diversified ETFs or funds if your goal is broad market exposure—not a collage of tiny stock bets.
- Cap trading frequency; schedule buys instead of tapping every pay day for sport.
- Add up yearly fees on fractional trades versus fewer, larger orders.
- Confirm the firm’s licence before depositing, and test a withdrawal early.
