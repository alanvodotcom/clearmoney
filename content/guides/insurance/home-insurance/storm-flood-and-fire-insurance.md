Storm, flood, and fire are the events Australians most associate with home claims—and the wording that decides them is technical. Two neighbours can receive opposite outcomes from the same weather system if one policy treats water as flood and the other as stormwater.

## Storm damage is not automatically flood damage

**Storm** sections often cover wind, hail, and rainwater that enters because of storm damage to the building. **Flood** is typically about water that escapes from a natural watercourse or similar defined source and inundates land. Hydrology reports after big events can reclassify what homeowners thought was “just a storm.”

If flood is excluded or optional and you declined it, a riverine inundation claim can fail even when the roof is fine. If you live on a floodplain, near a creek, or in a flash-flood suburb, treat flood inclusion as a core decision—not a footnote. Premiums rise with risk; that is the point of insurance pricing.

Actions of the sea, storm surge, and erosion may sit in separate clauses. Coastal households should read those lines explicitly.

## Fire and bushfire scenarios

Fire cover usually includes accidental fire and bushfire attack, subject to exclusions such as arson by the insured or heat without flame in some edge cases. Ember attack, smoke damage, and melt damage from nearby fires can be covered when wording allows—photograph and list them when you claim.

Bushfire-prone rebuilds may need higher construction standards than the home you lost. That raises rebuild cost and feeds [underinsurance](/topics/insurance/home-insurance/underinsurance-what-it-is-and-how-to-avoid-it) risk. Keep the sum insured honest in high-risk zones.

Some policies apply higher excesses for named cyclone events or earthquake. Know those excesses before season starts so cash flow plans are real.

## Excesses, waiting rules, and maintenance exclusions

Disaster claims still charge an excess—sometimes a special catastrophe excess. There is rarely a “waiting period” like income protection, but brand-new policies can face scrutiny if a claim lands immediately after inception during a forecast event; insurers watch anti-selection. Take out cover early, not the morning a cyclone warning issues, if you can avoid it.

Claims also fail when damage traces to long-term maintenance neglect: blocked gutters, rotten fascias, or known leaks left unrepaired. Insurers distinguish sudden storm damage from gradual water ingress. Keep maintenance records.

Temporary accommodation, debris removal, and garden or fencing limits vary widely—read them while calm. Preparation steps sit in [how to prepare for a natural disaster](/topics/insurance/natural-disasters/how-to-prepare-for-a-natural-disaster).

## Before the high-risk season

Pull your certificate of insurance and highlight flood status, fire cover, excess amounts, and temporary accommodation caps. Update sums insured. Ask the insurer how they treat stormwater backup from drains versus flood. If you are shopping policies, compare those definitions before price.


## Contents in garages, sheds, and downstairs flats

Water and fire do not respect the nice distinction between building and contents. Goods in garages and garden sheds often have lower sub-limits or extra exclusions. Ground-floor units can see stormwater and flood disputes even when upper floors stay dry.

If you store tools, sporting gear, or a freezer full of food outside the main living area, read those sub-limits now and raise them—or move high-value items before the season.

## What to do next

- Confirm whether flood is included, excluded, or optional on your current certificate.
- Note any cyclone, earthquake, or catastrophe excesses and save for them.
- Raise rebuild and contents sums if construction costs have moved—especially in bushfire zones.
- Clear gutters and fix known leaks so maintenance exclusions are harder to argue.
- Store policy PDFs off-site or in the cloud so you can claim even if the house is inaccessible.
