A cash settlement pays you money instead of the insurer arranging repairs or replacement. It can feel faster and more flexible. It also shifts project risk—quotes blowing out, dodgy builders, and incomplete scopes—onto you.

## When insurers offer cash

Insurers may propose cash when:

- you prefer to manage your own builder
- preferred repair networks are overloaded after a catastrophe
- the property will be sold or demolished anyway
- contents are easier to replace yourself than via a supplier panel
- liability or scope is partly disputed and a commercial settlement closes the file

You can also ask for cash. The insurer still calculates an amount tied to policy benefits, sum insured, and betterment or depreciation rules—not an open wish list.

Settle only after you understand whether the offer is full and final for that claim. Get the breakdown in writing: building labour and materials, contents lines, temporary accommodation remaining, and excess deducted.

## Rebuild quotes versus the cheque

Before you accept a building cash settlement, obtain independent quotes that match the insurer’s scope—or challenge the scope if rooms are missing. Include preliminaries, demolition, compliance upgrades, and contingency. If quotes exceed the settlement, you either negotiate, fund the gap, or reduce the rebuild specification.

Underinsurance makes cash settlements especially harsh: the policy limit caps the cheque even when real rebuild cost is higher. Fix sums before disasters where you can—[underinsurance guide](/topics/insurance/home-insurance/underinsurance-what-it-is-and-how-to-avoid-it).

Contents cash settlements should be checked line by line against your inventory. Bundled “room allowances” can hide underpayment on high-value items you could have specified on the policy.

## Responsibilities that move to you

Once you accept cash and the claim closes, you typically become the client of any builder you hire. That means contracts, progress payments, variation control, and defect fights are yours. Verify licences, insurance, and physical business addresses—disaster areas attract [chasers](/topics/insurance/natural-disasters/be-aware-of-disaster-chasers).

Do not sign over large deposits to door-to-door operators. Prefer staged payments tied to completed work. Keep the settlement funds segregated from everyday spending so the rebuild does not quietly fund other bills.

Tax, mortgagee consent, and strata approvals can all affect how money is used. If a bank holds a mortgage, ask whether they must be named on the settlement or must approve rebuilding plans.

## Repair-managed claims as the alternative

Letting the insurer manage repairs keeps contract risk with their builders and can include guarantees the insurer stands behind. Trade-off: less choice of finishes and timelines, and possible disputes over scope quality. Neither path is universally better; match it to your time, skill, and tolerance for project management.

If you are still early in the claim, revisit [how to make a home insurance claim](/topics/insurance/home-insurance/how-to-make-a-home-insurance-claim) before you lock a settlement path.


## Hidden damage and reopen questions

Ask in writing whether the settlement is full and final, and what happens if demolition reveals asbestos, termite damage, or structural issues not visible at assessment. Some insurers allow limited reopeners; others do not.

Hold a contingency slice of the settlement until demolition and framing expose surprises. Spending every dollar on finishes first is how rebuilds stall mid-project.

If you accept cash for contents, photograph serial numbers and keep replacement receipts in one folder. Insurers sometimes ask for proof that funds were used for like-for-like goods, especially on high-value electronics and jewellery.

## What to do next

- Request a written settlement breakdown and compare it to independent quotes on the same scope.
- Confirm whether acceptance closes the claim fully and what happens if hidden damage appears later.
- Check mortgagee or strata requirements before spending building funds.
- Vet any builder as carefully as you would for a major renovation—licence, insurance, staged payments.
- If the settlement reveals a sum-insured shortfall, fix cover on any property you still insure before the next season.
