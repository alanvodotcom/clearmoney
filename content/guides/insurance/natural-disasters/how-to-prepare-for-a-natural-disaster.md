Preparing for bushfire, flood, cyclone, or severe storm is partly physical and partly financial. Insurance only helps if the policy matches the risk and you can prove what you owned. Do that work on a clear weekend—not while water is rising.

## Know which hazards apply to your address

Check local council and emergency service maps for flood overlays, bushfire attack levels, and cyclone regions. Walk the property: Where does water pool? Are gutters clear? Is vegetation hard against the walls? Mitigation does not replace insurance, but it can prevent excluded “maintenance” arguments and reduce damage when an event hits.

Talk to your insurer about flood inclusion, bushfire cover, and any special excesses before peak season—definitions sit in [storm, flood and fire insurance](/topics/insurance/home-insurance/storm-flood-and-fire-insurance). If flood is excluded and you live in a flood-prone suburb, treat that as an open financial risk: build savings, plan alternative housing, or accept the gap deliberately rather than discovering it after inundation.

Renters still prepare: contents cover, a go-bag, and a plan for where you will sleep if the building is condemned. Building decisions sit with the landlord, but your devices and documents do not.

## Documents and inventories that survive the house

Create an encrypted cloud folder (and an offline USB kept at work or with family) containing:

- home and contents policy PDFs and certificates of currency
- identity documents, bank details, and mortgage or landlord contacts
- photos of every room, serial numbers, and big-ticket receipts
- medical scripts, vaccination records, and pet microchip numbers
- insurer claims numbers and a short contact list (insurer, council, out-of-area family)

Update the contents inventory after major purchases so [underinsurance](/topics/insurance/home-insurance/underinsurance-what-it-is-and-how-to-avoid-it) does not creep in. Photograph jewellery and bikes in situ. If you renovated, keep plans and builder invoices—they support a higher rebuild sum insured.

Paper in a kitchen drawer fails in a flood. Cloud access from a phone on mobile data is the recovery path that works when the house does not.

## Money access when power and branches fail

Keep a small amount of cash in a waterproof pouch; card networks and ATMs fail in outages. Confirm you can unlock online banking without a saved laptop left behind. Know whether you could pay an insurance excess this week from accessible funds—not only from a term deposit locked until next month.

Emergency kits belong in the same plan: water, medications, chargers, torches, batteries, important cables, N95 masks for ash or mould clean-up, and pet carriers. Physical readiness shortens the chaos that leads to bad financial decisions—like paying a stranger a huge cash deposit on day one.

If income would stop because your workplace sits in the same hazard zone, sketch a two-week cash flow: leave balances, disaster payments you might apply for, and which bills can pause under hardship.

## Household roles and seasonal triggers

Agree who grabs the document bag, who moves cars to higher ground or a safer suburb, and who calls elderly relatives. Set triggers tied to official warnings (“watch and act,” flood height posts, fire danger ratings) so you do not debate while leaving late. Neighbourhood phone trees beat improvisation when towers are congested.

Review sums insured and flood status each year as part of the same ritual—preparation includes making sure a future claim could actually rebuild. Store the new certificate in the cloud folder the same day it arrives.

## What to do next

- Confirm flood, fire, and excess details on your certificate; fix gaps before the season.
- Upload policies, IDs, and a photo inventory to cloud storage you can open off-site.
- Pack a go-bag and a small cash reserve; test banking access without home Wi-Fi.
- Walk through evacuation roles with everyone in the household, including pets.
- Diary a pre-season reminder so preparation does not depend on memory alone.
