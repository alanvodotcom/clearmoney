After floods, fires, and storms, unsolicited traders appear offering fast tarps, roof work, or full rebuilds. Some are legitimate locals helping. Others are disaster chasers: high-pressure sellers who want large deposits, vague contracts, and access to your insurance payout.

## Tactics that show up on the nature strip

Watch for:

- door-knocking the same day media arrive
- “we’re working on your neighbour’s roof right now” urgency
- requests for large cash deposits before materials arrive
- contracts with blank price fields or only a mobile number
- offers to “manage the insurer for you” in exchange for a cut
- pressure to sign before you speak to your claims consultant

Genuine make-safe work is usually coordinated through your insurer or a known local builder. If someone insists you must decide before sunset, that is a sales clock—not a weather clock.

## Verify before any money moves

Ask for:

- full business name and ABN
- physical business address you can confirm independently
- licence numbers relevant to your state for building or roofing work
- proof of public liability insurance
- a written scope and fixed or capped price
- staged payments tied to completed work—not 50% cash up front

Call the insurer before authorising major work. Unapproved repairs can complicate [home claims](/topics/insurance/home-insurance/how-to-make-a-home-insurance-claim). If you have a cash settlement, you still need licence checks—the risk simply sits with you; see [cash settlements](/topics/insurance/home-insurance/how-home-insurance-cash-settlements-work).

Search regulator registers and review sites for the exact entity name. Be wary of brand-new ABNs with no footprint after a catastrophe.

## Insurance and legal angle

You do not have to use a door-knocker to “speed up” a claim. Insurers have preferred networks and assessment steps for a reason. Signing a contract that assigns your claim benefits to a contractor can reduce your control—get advice before assigning rights.

If work is defective or the trader vanishes, gather the contract, receipts, photos, and messages. Complaints may go to state building regulators, consumer affairs, and, where an insurer is involved, the insurer’s process then AFCA. [How to complain](/topics/community/publications/how-to-complain) outlines the ladder.

Cooling-off rights can apply to some unsolicited consumer agreements—act quickly if you signed under pressure.

## Neighbourhood defence

Share known-good local trades through community groups once verified. Report aggressive chasers to local recovery hubs and police if fraud is suspected. Do not publish vulnerable neighbours’ claim details online.

Exhaustion makes everyone kinder to the first friendly face with a ladder. Build a rule in advance: no deposits on the day of door-knock; sleep on it; call the insurer first.


## If you already paid a deposit

Stop further payments. Photograph the site, the incomplete work, and every message. Ask your bank about chargebacks or scam processes if the trader used a card. Notify your insurer so they know an unapproved contractor was involved.

Contact state building or consumer regulators with the ABN and contract. Free legal advice services and financial counsellors can help sequence next steps when the deposit was large. Shame delays recovery—chasers rely on that.

Community Facebook groups can help—and can also amplify unverified traders. Prefer recommendations that include a licence number you can check yourself, not just a first name and a van photo.

## What to do next

- Tell household members the “no same-day deposit” rule before the next season.
- Channel make-safe and repairs through your insurer unless you have verified credentials independently.
- Check ABN, licences, and insurance on any trade you consider after a disaster.
- Refuse blank contracts and benefit assignments you do not understand.
- If already caught by a chaser, stop further payments, document everything, and escalate to regulators and your insurer promptly.
