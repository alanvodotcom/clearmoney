Income protection is designed to replace a slice of your earnings if illness or injury stops you working for a while. Unlike a death benefit, the claim is about cash flow month to month—so waiting periods, benefit periods, and how “unable to work” is defined matter as much as the headline replacement percentage.

## What the benefit is meant to do

Most policies pay a monthly benefit—often up to about 70% of insured income—while you meet the disability definition. The money is meant for living costs and ongoing bills, not to clear a mortgage in one hit. That is why [life cover](/topics/insurance/life-insurance/life-insurance-cover) and [TPD](/topics/insurance/life-insurance/total-and-permanent-disability-tpd-insurance) solve different problems.

Cover can sit inside super or outside it. Inside super, premiums may feel “invisible” because they come from your balance, but benefit design and tax treatment can differ. Outside super, you usually pay premiums from after-tax cash and may have different claim flexibility. Check both before you assume you are unprotected—or over-insured.

## Waiting periods and benefit periods

Two dials control almost every quote:

**Waiting period** — how long you must be off work before benefits start (commonly 30, 60, or 90 days; longer waits usually mean cheaper premiums). Match this to sick leave, annual leave, and an emergency buffer. A 90-day wait is a false economy if you have two weeks of leave and no savings.

**Benefit period** — how long payments can continue (for example two years, five years, or to a set age). A short benefit period costs less but leaves a cliff if recovery takes longer. A long period costs more and needs a clear view of what you would do if you never return to the same job.

Some policies offset other income— Centrelink, workers compensation, or ongoing sick pay—so the insurer pays less. Model a claim with those offsets included, not on the brochure’s best-case figure.

## Definitions that decide whether you get paid

“Own occupation” definitions generally ask whether you can do your usual job. “Any occupation” definitions ask whether you can do work you are reasonably suited for by education and experience. The second is typically harder to satisfy and often cheaper.

Also check:

- **Partial disability** — whether reduced hours still attract a partial benefit.
- **Exclusions** — pre-existing conditions, self-inflicted injury, war, and specific sports or hazardous work.
- **Indemnity vs agreed value style wording** — whether the insurer recalculates benefit from recent earnings at claim time.
- **Premium structure** — stepped vs level, and whether cover continues if you change employers.

Answer health and income questions accurately. Understating income can shrink a future benefit; omitting conditions can undermine the whole claim.

## When income protection is usually a poor fit

If you already have a long paid leave arrangement, large liquid savings, and low fixed costs, you may need less cover. If your income is irregular, confirm how the insurer measures “pre-disability earnings” for contractors and casuals. Stacking multiple overlapping policies rarely helps once offsets apply—coordinate super and retail cover instead of duplicating them blindly.

Income protection does not replace trauma cover’s lump sum for medical and lifestyle costs, and it is not a substitute for clearing debts on death. Keep the product jobs separate.

## What to do next

- Map sick leave and cash buffer, then choose a waiting period you can actually fund.
- Compare benefit periods and own-occupation vs any-occupation wording in the PDS—not only the monthly premium.
- List existing cover in [super](/topics/insurance/life-insurance/insurance-through-super) and any retail policies so offsets do not surprise you.
- Confirm how income is calculated for your employment type and what exclusions apply to your work or hobbies.
- If a permanent inability to work is the bigger fear, read [TPD insurance](/topics/insurance/life-insurance/total-and-permanent-disability-tpd-insurance) alongside this guide.
