Identity theft means someone uses your personal details—name, date of birth, address, tax file number fragments, or ID documents—to open accounts, take credit, or hijack services in your name. Speed matters more than perfection. If bills or debts you did not create are already threatening essentials, use [Urgent help](/urgent).

## Signs someone is using your details

Watch for patterns, not one odd email:

- Bank, tax, or Centrelink messages about applications you did not make
- Mail or SMS for credit cards, loans, or SIMs you never ordered
- Password-reset emails for accounts you still control—or for accounts you forgot existed
- Collection calls for debts you do not recognise
- myGov or Medicare activity you did not initiate

Also treat data-breach notices seriously. A breach does not always mean misuse, but it raises the odds. Change passwords on affected and reused logins promptly.

## Lock down accounts in the first hour

Work from money out to identity in:

1. **Contact banks and card issuers** on numbers from cards or statements. Report suspected fraud, cancel compromised cards, and ask about new account alerts.
2. **Change passwords** on email first (it resets everything else), then banking, myGov, and super. Turn on multi-factor authentication you control. Never share OTPs with callers who claim they are “verifying your identity.”
3. **Secure the phone number** — SIM-swap fraud uses your number to intercept codes. Contact your mobile provider if messages stop arriving or a new SIM appears.
4. **Revoke suspicious app access** and sign out other sessions where platforms allow it.

If remote-access software was installed during a “bank security” call, disconnect from the internet, uninstall it, and tell the bank the device may have been compromised.

## Credit files and government credentials

Order your free credit reports from the major bureaus and dispute entries you did not open. See [Credit scores and credit reports](/topics/loans-credit-debt/managing-debt/credit-scores-and-credit-reports) for how files work. Ask about credit reporting bans or alerts if misuse is confirmed.

For lost or stolen physical ID, follow the issuer’s replacement process (licence, passport). Tell the ATO and myGov if tax or government identity may be involved—use official channels you type yourself, not links from panic emails.

Keep a dated log: who you called, reference numbers, and what was frozen. That log helps when you dispute debts later.

## Report, then rebuild calmly

- Report identity misuse and related scams to [Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au).
- Report cyber incidents (hacking, malware, account takeover) through [ReportCyber](https://www.cyber.gov.au/report-and-recover/report).
- If money was taken by deception, also follow [What to do if you’ve been scammed](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/what-to-do-if-youve-been-scammed).

Recovery is often weeks of follow-up, not one phone call. Prioritise stopping new credit in your name, then cleaning the file. Do not pay “identity repair” cold callers who demand upfront fees—that can be a second scam. Real help uses channels you initiate, and it never needs your banking OTP.

Tell employers or payroll if someone may try to redirect your salary. Update myGov and Medicare login security the same day you secure banking. Prevention still helps next time: unique passwords, careful document disposal, and the habits in [Protect yourself from scams](/topics/scams-safety/online-safety/protect-yourself-from-scams).

Check mail redirection and electoral roll changes if someone may be intercepting letters with new credit contracts in your name.

## What to do next

- If misuse is active now, call your bank and mobile provider before you finish reading other guides.
- Request credit reports this week and diary a follow-up check in 30 days.
- Report via Scamwatch and ReportCyber with dates, accounts, and evidence attached.
- If debt collectors are already contacting you about fraudulent accounts and cash is tight, open [Urgent help](/urgent).
