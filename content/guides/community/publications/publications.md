ClearMoney publications are short, topic-focused tip sheets. They explain everyday Australian money decisions in plain language. They are general information only—not personal advice, and not affiliated with ASIC or any product issuer.

## What you will find in this hub

Each guide targets one job: paying bills on time, spotting a scam pattern, understanding a payslip line, or knowing how a complaint usually escalates. Articles sit under community publications so you can skim by need rather than wade through a textbook.

Related guides at the bottom of each page point sideways—budgeting beside bills, scams beside complaints—so you can deepen one step at a time. Tools such as budget planners appear where a calculator helps more than another paragraph.

Thresholds, rates, and government settings change. Treat any dollar example as a prompt to check the current official figure before you apply, claim, or sign.

## How to read a tip sheet without overloading

Start with the lead: it states the problem the page actually solves. Skim the headings; jump to the section that matches your week. Finish on **What to do next**—those bullets are the intended takeaway.

Ignore sales language elsewhere on the web that borrows education-looking layouts. ClearMoney will not ask you to buy a financial product from a tip sheet. If a pop-up, text, or caller claims to be us and wants remote access or payment, stop and verify independently—see [beware of scams](/topics/community/publications/beware-of-scams).

Print or save a PDF only of pages you will use offline; outdated printouts mislead. Prefer the live page when a number decides an application.

Students have a separate hub for cash-flow topics such as cars, phones, and rent. Use student guides when the constraint is variable shifts and semester timing; use publications when you want a compact tip sheet.

If money stress threatens housing, energy, or safety this week, go to [Urgent help](/urgent) first. Tip sheets assume you have enough stability to plan; crisis pathways come first when you do not.

## Share guides without spreading stale rules

When you forward a link to family, include the date and say “check the current rule on the official site.” Concession cards, contribution caps, and scam typologies shift. Children can help relatives open a webpage; they should not negotiate loans or hardship calls—use professional interpreters when needed.

Community educators can use tip sheets as session handouts if they add local contacts (financial counselling, legal aid, settlement services). Do not present ClearMoney text as a regulator publication.

## Keeping your own notes beside the guides

A tip sheet works best next to your numbers: balances, due dates, policy numbers, complaint references. One folder—digital or paper—beats scattered screenshots. After you act, write what you did and when you will review. That personal note ages better than another bookmark.

Revisit topics after life changes: new job, baby, separation, move, or a large inheritance. The same heading can mean a different action when cash flow changes.
