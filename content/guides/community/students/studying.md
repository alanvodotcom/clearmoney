Study years rarely come with a steady full-time wage. The money skill that matters most is matching a lumpy income to fixed costs—rent, transport, food, devices—without leaning on high-cost credit when a semester gets tight.

## Cash flow while you study

Map money by **teaching weeks and breaks**, not by a tidy monthly salary fantasy. Many students get:

- Casual or part-time wages that spike in holidays and drop in exam blocks
- Youth Allowance, Austudy, or Abstudy paid on Centrelink cycles
- Parental help that arrives irregularly
- Scholarship or grant payments once or twice a year

Write a two-column list: money that arrives on a known date versus money that might arrive. Build the rent and essentials plan on the known column only. Treat the “might” column as bonus that clears debt or tops savings—not as money you spend before it lands.

A simple [budget planner](/tools/budget-planner) helps you see the gap in dollars, not vibes.

## HECS-HELP is not free cash

HECS-HELP and similar student loans defer tuition. They are not spending money. Indexation and repayment rules change over time—use the [HECS calculator](/tools/hecs) and official StudyAssist or ATO explanations when you need current thresholds.

Practical habits:

- Know which units are covered and what you still pay upfront (student services, textbooks, equipment)
- Keep enrolment documents so you can check debts later in myGov
- Remember repayments usually start when your income crosses a threshold—plan for that when you move into full-time work

Borrowing more HELP than you need for lifestyle spending is a trap; HELP is for eligible course costs, not festivals.

## Income support and part-time work

If you receive income support, report employment income on time. Under-reporting creates debts; over-cutting shifts can leave you short for rent. Read Centrelink letters fully—especially mutual obligation and cut-off dates around study load changes.

When you pick up shifts, estimate **take-home** pay, not the rostered hourly headline. Tax and possible support reductions both matter. See [getting a job](/topics/community/students/getting-a-job) for payslip basics.

If hours swing wildly, the [managing on a casual income](/topics/banking-budgeting/budgeting/managing-on-a-casual-income) guide matches student life better than a classic 9-to-5 budget.

## A term budget that survives exam weeks

Build three layers:

1. **Floor costs** — rent/board, transport to uni, basic food, phone, essential medications
2. **Study costs** — texts, printing, software, lab gear, placement travel
3. **Buffer** — a small amount for the week an extra shift disappears

Before SWOT vac, move money into a bills account so you are not relying on shifts you will cancel to revise. Freeze non-essentials deliberately for that fortnight rather than discovering the shortfall mid-exam.

If credit cards or BNPL are filling gaps, stop and read [credit and debt for students](/topics/community/students/credit-and-debt) before the balances compound.

## Placement and prac costs

Teaching, nursing, and trade placements can add travel, uniforms, and lost shift income. Ask your course office early about scholarships, placement bursaries, or timetable clashes with work. Build a placement mini-budget so the semester you “have no shifts” does not become a credit-card semester.

Build a tiny textbook contingency each term—even a few dollars a week—so an unexpected software licence does not land on a credit card.

## What to do next

- List every income source and whether the date is reliable; budget essentials on reliable income only.
- Run your HELP situation through the [HECS tool](/tools/hecs) and note any upfront costs your loan will not cover.
- Draft a term budget in the [budget planner](/tools/budget-planner), including an exam-week version with fewer shifts.
- Confirm Centrelink reporting rules if you work.
- Read [moving out of home](/topics/community/students/moving-out-of-home) before you sign a lease on a student income.
