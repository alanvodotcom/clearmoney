These activity outlines are starting points you can stretch or shrink to a single period. Each one centres on a decision students recognise, uses a document or scenario, and ends with a transferable check—not a product recommendation.

## Building a lesson around a real money choice

**Learning intention example:** Students compare two offers and justify a choice using total cost and conditions—not advertising slogans.

**Flow (45–60 minutes):**

1. **Hook (5 min)** — Show two anonymised phone-plan screenshots or supermarket receipts with different unit prices.
2. **Teach the lens (10 min)** — Define *total cost*, *ongoing fee*, and *condition* (for example “bonus rate if you deposit $X”).
3. **Guided practice (15 min)** — Pairs fill a three-column table: headline claim / fine print / what it means in dollars per month.
4. **Independent or group task (15 min)** — Students choose Plan A or B for a fictional student with a stated weekly income.
5. **Exit ticket (5 min)** — One sentence: “I would pick ___ because ___.”

Swap phone plans for buy-now-pay-later schedules or savings account bonus rules. The structure stays; the artefact changes. Background reading for older students: [choosing a mobile phone plan](/topics/community/students/choosing-a-mobile-phone-plan).

## Needs versus wants (and why the binary breaks)

Younger classes often meet “needs vs wants” as a sorting game. Push one step further so it stays honest.

**Activity:** Give groups a weekly budget for a fictional teen and a list of expenses (transport, lunch, data, birthday gift, streaming, sports fees). Ask them to categorise, then force a cut of 20% when “income” falls.

**Discussion prompts:**

- Which “want” protects wellbeing (sport, seeing friends)?
- Which “need” has a cheaper substitute?
- Who decides needs in a family—and how does that feel?

Avoid ranking students’ real lives. Keep the household fictional. The learning goal is trade-offs under a constraint, not moralising consumption.

## Scams as critical thinking practice

Treat scam lessons as media literacy, not fear theatre.

**Activity:** Students annotate a mock SMS or social post: sender, urgency language, secrecy request, money movement asked for, how to verify independently.

**Success criteria:** Students can explain *one* safe verification step (for example navigate to the bank site manually, not via the message link) and *one* reporting path appropriate to their age group.

Keep examples age-suitable. For teacher context and community tip language, see [beware of scams](/topics/community/publications/beware-of-scams). Emphasise that anyone can be targeted—shame shuts down reporting.

## Assessment without shaming household income

Prefer tasks that assess process:

- Rubrics on “uses evidence from the fine print”
- Reflection on a fictional case study
- Creation of a poster that teaches a peer one check before paying online

Avoid graded budgets that require real family figures. If you use journals, make private finances optional and ungraded.

For senior students touching credit, keep it definitional and cash-flow based—link to [credit and debt for students](/topics/community/students/credit-and-debt) as extension reading, not as a homework audit of their parents’ cards.

## Differentiation and extension

For advanced students, add a total-cost spreadsheet task. For supported learners, pre-highlight fine-print sentences and ask for oral explanations. Same artefact, different output—keeps the class together without exposing private finances.

Invite a guest only when they teach a skill (reading a contract) rather than pitching a product. Brief them on your no-sales rule in writing beforehand.

## What to do next

- Pick one outline above and swap in an artefact from your subject (receipt, ad, payslip, plan).
- Write a fictional learner profile so discussion stays safe.
- Align the exit ticket to a curriculum outcome you already report on.
- Read [teaching consumer and financial literacy](/topics/community/teachers/teaching-consumer-and-financial-literacy) for framing and family communication tips.
- Store a short list of school wellbeing contacts in your lesson notes if disclosures arise.
