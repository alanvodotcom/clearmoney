Money problems are easier to face when the person across the table understands community context. Across Australia there are Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander organisations, Indigenous-focused financial counselling programs, and mainstream services that offer culturally safer pathways—if you know where to start.

## Culturally safe money help exists

“Culturally safe” here means you can talk about family obligations, remote access barriers, sorry business costs, and mistrust of institutions without being dismissed. Look for:

- Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisations (ACCOs) with financial wellbeing or emergency relief programs
- Indigenous-specific financial counselling or money-management projects in your region
- Legal services with First Nations clients’ practices for consumer and tenancy issues
- Local land councils or community centres that can refer you to the right worker

Ask at the front desk: “Who helps with Centrelink, banking, or debt?” One warm referral beats cold-calling a national hotline when you need a familiar face.

## Financial counselling and community organisations

Financial counsellors are free and confidential. They can help prioritise bills, contact lenders, and plan around income. National Debt Helpline 1800 007 007 can connect you; ask whether an Indigenous-identified or culturally trained counsellor is available in your area.

Community organisations may also offer:

- Help opening accounts or replacing ID
- Emergency relief for food or power
- Support navigating book-up, funeral costs, or compensation payments

Bring whatever paperwork you have—letters, texts from lenders, Centrelink screenshots. Workers can sort a messy pile with you. Broader context: [financial counselling](/topics/loans-credit-debt/managing-debt/financial-counselling).

## Government and regulator contacts that still matter

Alongside community services, keep official doors on your list:

- Services Australia / Centrelink for payments and reporting
- Your bank’s hardship team for repayment difficulties
- AFCA for unresolved complaints about financial firms
- ASIC and Scamwatch information for scams and unlicensed lenders

Using a community worker *and* these systems together is normal. The worker helps you navigate; the institution still holds the account or debt.

## Choosing who you talk to first

Match the door to the problem:

- **Hungry tonight / power cut risk** → emergency relief or thrift supports first
- **Debt collectors / unaffordable loans** → financial counsellor
- **Bank account or ID barriers** → community worker plus [bank accounts](/topics/community/first-nations/bank-accounts) and [how to prove your identity](/topics/community/first-nations/how-to-prove-your-identity)
- **Family money pressure** → trusted counsellor or family violence service if safety is involved—see [dealing with family pressure about money](/topics/community/first-nations/dealing-with-family-pressure-about-money)

If one service cannot help, ask them to name the next place—not just “search online.”

## What to bring to a first appointment

Arrive with whatever you have—even a messy envelope beats nothing:

- Recent Centrelink or wage screenshots
- Letters from lenders, energy companies, or debt collectors
- Book-up or store account notes
- A list of who depends on your income
- Questions written in your own words

You do not need perfect English or a complete file. Workers are used to starting mid-story. If transport or childcare is a barrier, ask whether phone appointments or outreach days exist. Taking a support person is fine; say if you want them in the room for the whole meeting or only the start.

## Following up without losing heart

Services are busy. If a callback does not arrive, ring once more with your name and the date you first called. Ask for the next outreach day or another organisation’s number. Persistence is part of the system—not a sign you did something wrong. Keep a small notebook of contacts so you do not restart from zero each time.

## What to do next

- Ask your local ACCO or community centre which worker helps with money, bills, or Centrelink.
- Call 1800 007 007 and ask about culturally appropriate financial counselling near you.
- Write a one-page list of debts, income, and urgent bills before your appointment.
- For high-cost lending pressure, read [cash loans](/topics/community/first-nations/cash-loans) before you sign anything new.
- Keep one trusted contact’s number on paper in case your phone credit runs out.
