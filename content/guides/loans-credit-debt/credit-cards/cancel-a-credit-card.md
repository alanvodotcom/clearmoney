Cancelling a credit card is straightforward mechanically and slightly nuanced for your credit file and bill autopays. Close cards you do not need—especially high-fee or tempting ones—once the balance is handled and payments are redirected.

## Before you close: pay down and unlink

Work through a pre-close checklist:

1. **Pay the balance to zero** (or transfer it deliberately as part of a plan).
2. **List recurring charges** — streaming, utilities, gym, software, donations. Move each to a debit account or another card and confirm one successful payment.
3. **Download statements** for your records (tax, warranties, disputes).
4. **Redeem or forfeit points** knowingly—some points vanish when the account closes.
5. **Note annual fee timing** — closing shortly after a fee posts can be costly; ask if a fee rebate is possible when you cancel.

Skipping the direct-debit hunt is how people discover a failed payment and a late fee on a “closed” product that was not fully shut—or worse, a service interruption. See [direct debits](/topics/banking-budgeting/banking/direct-debits).

## How closing can affect your credit file

Credit reports and scoring models may consider:

- length of credit history
- utilisation (balances versus total limits)
- number of open accounts

Closing an old card can shorten average account age or raise utilisation if other cards still carry balances. That does not mean you must keep every card forever. Practical approach:

- If you have multiple cards, consider keeping the oldest no-fee (or low-fee) card open with a low limit and almost no use.
- Close high-fee rewards cards you no longer justify.
- If a home-loan application is weeks away, ask your broker or lender whether closing cards now helps or hurts serviceability—unused limits can weigh on borrowing capacity even when balances are low.

Accurate information stays on file for set periods; closing a card does not erase past late payments. See [credit scores and credit reports](/topics/loans-credit-debt/managing-debt/credit-scores-and-credit-reports).

## Steps to cancel cleanly

1. Confirm the balance is zero and no pending authorisations remain.
2. Call the issuer or use their secure cancellation path; ask for a **written confirmation** with date and time.
3. Destroy the physical card.
4. Watch your email/mail for final statements.
5. Check your credit report a few weeks later to ensure the account shows closed as expected.

If the issuer tries a hard retain offer (fee waivers, bonus points), accept only if it matches the behaviour you want—not if it restarts overspending.

## Keeping one card for emergencies

Many people keep a single low-limit card in a drawer (or locked in the app) for genuine emergencies: essential car repairs away from home, urgent bookings, or fraud separation from everyday debit. Rules that keep that safe:

- no everyday spending on it
- automatic full-balance repayment if it is ever used
- limit low enough that a worst-case binge is survivable

If card debt is already unmanageable, focus on repayment and hardship help first—[/urgent](/urgent) and National Debt Helpline **1800 007 007**—before optimising which plastic to keep.

## After cancellation: watch for zombies

Occasionally a final fee posts, a merchant retries a charge, or a “retention” courtesy line reopens access you thought was dead. Check the account status online a week later and again after the next statement cycle. If anything reappears, call back with your cancellation reference. Update password managers and remove the card from digital wallets, rideshare apps, and online stores so old credentials cannot resurrect spending.

## What to do next

- Move every recurring payment off the card you plan to close.
- Zero the balance and request written cancellation confirmation.
- Keep one low-fee, low-limit card only if you have a clear emergency rule.
- Review your credit report after closing to confirm the status looks right.
