A savings account is a place to park money you do not want to spend by accident. The rate matters—but conditions and access rules often matter more.

## What makes a savings account different?

Savings accounts usually pay higher interest than everyday accounts and may limit easy spending. Some are “online only”, some need a linked transaction account, and many advertise a **bonus rate** that lasts only while you meet monthly conditions (deposit amount, no withdrawals, number of card purchases elsewhere).

If you can spend from the savings account with a debit card, it is functionally another spending account with a nicer name. Prefer accounts where spending is inconvenient on purpose.

## Bonus rates and the fine print

### Conditions that cancel the bonus

Common traps: missing a minimum deposit, making a withdrawal, or failing to grow the balance. Casual income months break “deposit every month” rules easily. Read what rate applies when conditions fail—and for how long the intro bonus lasts.

### Multiple accounts

Some people open several high-interest accounts for different goals (emergency, holiday, deposit). That can help mentally—but tracking conditions across five products becomes a part-time job. Two or three named goals usually beat a dozen forgotten logins.

## Benefits of separating savings

- **Speed bump** — money is less likely to disappear on tap-and-go.
- **Clear goals** — nicknames (“Emergency”, “House”) reduce guilt-free raiding.
- **Interest** — even modest rates beat leaving surplus in a zero-interest transaction account.
- **Automation** — payday transfers build balances without willpower.

Use the [Savings goals](/tools/savings-goals) calculator if you want a target date and weekly amount on paper.

Before you open anything, write one sentence: “This money is for ___ by ___.” If you cannot finish the sentence, you are collecting balances, not goals—and balances without goals are easiest to spend.

## A short story: chasing 0.2%

Priya moved savings three times in a year for tiny rate bumps, each time risking a missed direct debit on the linked account. She settled on one competitive savings account, automated 10% of pay, and reviewed the rate twice a year instead of twice a month. Progress became steadier than the chase.

If you still hold high-interest credit card or personal loan debt, compare the interest you would avoid by repaying against the interest you would earn by saving. A 20% debt usually beats a 4% savings rate as a first move—then rebuild the buffer once the expensive balance shrinks.

## How to set one up well

1. Name the goal and rough target before you open the account.
2. Compare **base rate**, **bonus rate**, conditions, and bonus end date.
3. Automate a transfer on payday; treat it like rent.
4. Lock or avoid a debit card on the savings account.
5. Calendar the bonus expiry and any condition you might fail on a quiet income month.

## When life changes

- **High-interest debt** — paying expensive debt often beats collecting a small savings rate; run both numbers.
- **Joint goals** — if two people save together, agree contributions and access; see [Joint accounts](/topics/banking-budgeting/banking/joint-accounts).
- **Emergency use** — know how long withdrawals take and whether they kill the bonus.

## What to do next

- Open or rename one savings account after a real goal.
- Automate a payday transfer—even a small one.
- Check [How to start saving](/topics/banking-budgeting/saving/how-to-start-saving) and [Save for an emergency fund](/topics/banking-budgeting/saving/save-for-an-emergency-fund).
- Keep day-to-day spending in a [transaction account](/topics/banking-budgeting/banking/transaction-accounts-and-debit-cards), not in the goal balance.
