Sending money overseas is rarely only about the advertised fee. The **exchange rate margin**, speed, and who receives the funds decide the real cost—and whether a “helpful” stranger is actually a scam.

## How international transfers work

You instruct a bank or remittance provider to pay someone abroad in their currency (or yours). Providers make money from:

- **Upfront fees** (fixed or percentage)
- **Exchange rate markup** (the quiet cost)
- **Speed tiers** (same day costs more than slower rails)

Some transfers use SWIFT bank-to-bank rails; others use specialised remittance networks. Recipient details must be exact—wrong account numbers can delay or misroute funds.

## Compare the real cost

1. Ask for the **amount the recipient will receive** in their currency, not only your send amount.
2. Note the fee and the exchange rate used.
3. Check how long delivery takes and whether intermediate banks can deduct charges.
4. Confirm identification and daily limits before you need an urgent send.

A “$0 fee” transfer with a weak exchange rate can cost more than a transfer with a clear fee and a stronger rate.

For family support transfers you send often, save the beneficiary once you have confirmed details offline, then reuse that record. Re-typing long account numbers each time is how honest mistakes happen.

## Safety checks before you send

- **Know the recipient** — if you have not met them offline, slow down hard.
- **Verify independently** — call the person or business on a known number; do not use details from an unexpected email.
- **Ignore urgency + secrecy** — “send now or the deal dies” and “don’t tell your bank” are classic scam scripts.
- **Watch investment and romance pitches** that require crypto or repeated overseas transfers.

If you think you are being scammed, stop and use [Urgent help](/urgent) / scam response guides before sending more. Do not send a second “verification” payment to unlock the first—that pattern almost never ends well.

## A short story: the invoice that almost worked

Chris received a supplier invoice with new bank details “because of an audit”. The email looked right. Chris called the supplier’s main switchboard from the company website, not the email signature, and learned the request was fake. One phone call protected a five-figure transfer.

## Practical setup tips

1. Save beneficiary details carefully after the first successful transfer.
2. Start with a small test amount for a new recipient when timing allows.
3. Keep receipts and reference numbers until the recipient confirms funds.
4. Prefer providers you can contact easily if something goes wrong.
5. Never grant remote access to your computer so someone can “help” you transfer.

## When life changes

- **Supporting family overseas** — automate what you can, but review amounts when FX moves sharply.
- **Buying property or tuition abroad** — confirm institutional details through official portals.
- **Mistaken send** — contact the provider immediately; recovery is not guaranteed.

## What to do next

- Price one real transfer two ways (bank vs specialist) using the recipient-gets amount.
- Bookmark official contact channels for your bank and remittance provider.
- Read [Unauthorised and mistaken transactions](/topics/banking-budgeting/banking/unauthorised-and-mistaken-transactions) so you know the dispute path.
- If pressure appears, stop and check [What to do if you’ve been scammed](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/what-to-do-if-youve-been-scammed).
