Money leaving your account without your OK—or landing in the wrong place—is stressful. Speed, evidence, and the right contact channel matter more than perfect wording.

## Unauthorised vs mistaken

**Unauthorised** usually means someone else used your card, account, or credentials without your permission (stolen card, phishing, account takeover).

**Mistaken** often means you sent money to the wrong account, paid the wrong amount, or a biller charged after you cancelled. Some cases sit in between—like a child using a saved card, or a merchant double-charging.

Your rights and the bank’s process differ by type. Be clear which one you think it is when you report.

Banks investigate faster when you provide a clean timeline and avoid arguing about blame in the first call. Lead with facts: amount, date, channel (card, transfer, debit), and whether you still have the device or card.

## What to do immediately

1. **Freeze the card or block the channel** in your banking app if fraud is possible.
2. **Call the bank** using a number from the card, statement, or official website—not from a text or pop-up.
3. **Change passwords** starting with email, then banking, then any linked wallets.
4. **Write a timeline** — dates, amounts, merchants, reference numbers, screenshots.
5. **Ask for a dispute or investigation reference** and keep it.

If a scam is involved, also follow [What to do if you’ve been scammed](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/what-to-do-if-youve-been-scammed) and consider [Urgent help](/urgent). Shame delays recovery—report even if you feel foolish; scams are designed to create that feeling.

## Direct debits and recurring charges

If a cancelled subscription still charges, cancel again in writing, keep proof, and ask the bank about stopping the authority. Review [Direct debits](/topics/banking-budgeting/banking/direct-debits) so the same biller cannot quietly return.

## Mistaken payments you sent

Contact your bank quickly with the amount, time, and intended versus actual recipient details. Recovery may require the receiving bank’s cooperation and is not guaranteed—especially if funds were withdrawn. Do not send more money because someone claims a “release fee” will fix it; that is a common follow-on scam.

## A short story: the hold that looked like theft

Riley saw a large hotel-shaped hold the morning after booking travel and assumed fraud. The bank explained a temporary authorisation; the final charge later matched the booking. Riley still turned on alerts so the next surprise was a notification, not a panic spiral—and learned to read pending versus posted transactions.

## How to reduce the chance it happens again

- Turn on transaction alerts for amounts you care about.
- Do not share one-time codes or remote-access “support” sessions.
- Use unique passwords and multi-factor authentication on email and banking.
- Separate everyday spend from long-term savings so a compromised card hits a smaller balance.
- Review card-on-file merchants quarterly.

## When life changes

- **Shared accounts** — agree who monitors alerts; either-to-operate access raises the stakes ([Joint accounts](/topics/banking-budgeting/banking/joint-accounts)).
- **Lost phone** — freeze cards and banking app access immediately.
- **Travel** — tell the bank and watch for unusual foreign transactions.

## What to do next

- Enable purchase and low-balance alerts today.
- Save your bank’s official dispute phone number offline.
- Skim [Protect yourself from scams](/topics/scams-safety/online-safety/protect-yourself-from-scams) and [Identity theft](/topics/scams-safety/online-safety/identity-theft).
- Keep PDFs of any cancellation confirmations for subscriptions you end.
