Retirees hold visible assets, often answer the phone at home, and may feel time-rich when a friendly caller offers a "once-only" investment. Those traits attract criminals and high-pressure sales teams alike. Protection is mostly about habits and household rules—not clever security products sold over the phone.

If you think a scam is in motion, stop contact and use [Urgent help](/urgent): call your bank on the number on your card, not on a text message. ClearMoney offers general information—not personal advice.

## Why retirees are targeted

Scammers know many retirees control super lump sums, home equity, and savings built over decades. Cognitive changes, bereavement, and isolation increase susceptibility—not because victims are foolish, but because criminals rehearse scripts that exploit trust and urgency.

Pushy super switching calls, fake government threats, and romance scams migrating to money requests all spike in later life. Assume inbound contact is hostile until verified independently.

## Scams that show up often

High-frequency themes in Australia include:

- **Fake bank, ATO, or Centrelink calls** demanding one-time codes or remote access
- **Investment "sure things"**—crypto, foreign exchange, and recovery scams that promise to return money you already lost
- **Romance scams** that start on social media and end with wire transfers
- **Super switching cold calls**—see [Protect your super from pushy sales calls](/topics/super-retirement/grow-super/protect-your-super-from-pushy-sales-calls)
- **Grandparent emergencies** requiring secrecy, cash, or gift cards
- **Phishing emails** with links to fake myGov or bank login pages

Broader guides: [Protect yourself from scams](/topics/scams-safety/online-safety/protect-yourself-from-scams) and [Investment scams](/topics/scams-safety/financial-scams/investment-scams).

## Household rules that work

Agree these with your partner, housemate, or a trusted neighbour—and practise them:

1. **No money decisions on inbound calls.** Hang up; call back on a number you look up.
2. **No remote-access software** for strangers claiming to "fix your computer" or "secure your account."
3. **Verify large transfers** with a second person after an offline check—call the recipient on a known number.
4. **Bookmark official login sites.** Never use links from SMS or email to reach myGov or banking.
5. **Cool-off period** for any seminar pitch—sleep on it at least one night; a fortnight for large sums.

Write the rules on the fridge. Role-play once: "Hi, I'm from the bank fraud team…" so refusal feels natural.

## Banks and transaction alerts

Turn on SMS or app alerts for transactions above a low threshold. Tell your bank if you travel or expect unusual transfers. Ask what confirmation protocols exist for large OSKO or pay-anyone payments—some banks allow delay windows if you call quickly.

If something feels wrong mid-transfer, call the fraud line immediately from the number on your card—not from the message that triggered panic.

## Advisers and sales pressure

Check licence status on the [financial advisers register](https://moneysmart.gov.au/financial-advice/financial-advisers-register) before paying for advice. Prefer written advice with fees disclosed upfront. Free barbecues that end in signature packs are a warning pattern, not a community service.

Legitimate advisers do not demand immediate transfers to unfamiliar accounts or cryptocurrency wallets. If an "opportunity" cannot wait for your solicitor or accountant to read the paperwork, it can wait forever.

Treat unexpected inheritances, lottery wins, and overseas windfalls in unsolicited messages as scams until proven otherwise—real executors use post and known solicitors, not WhatsApp urgency.

## If money has already gone

Speed matters:

1. Contact your bank or fund fraud line immediately
2. Report to [Scamwatch](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au) and [ReportCyber](https://www.cyber.gov.au/acsc/report)
3. Change passwords on email and banking; enable multi-factor authentication
4. Consider ID monitoring if documents were shared
5. Talk to someone you trust—shame helps criminals; early reporting helps recovery attempts

Guide: [What to do if you've been scammed](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/what-to-do-if-youve-been-scammed). Recovery is not guaranteed, but silence guarantees nothing.

## Romance and companionship scams

Loneliness is not a character flaw—criminals know it. Be wary of online relationships that avoid video calls, declare love quickly, and eventually request money for emergencies, investments, or travel to meet you. Tell a friend about new online companions; secrecy is part of the scam design.

## Investment seminar circuit

Free lunches that pitch property, wind farms, or exotic bonds deserve the same scepticism as cold calls. Take the brochure home; check the [Investor alert list](/topics/scams-safety/check-report/investor-alert-list); do not sign at the venue. Real opportunities survive a week of verification.

## Family and carer financial abuse

Not all threats are strangers. Pressure to sign blank withdrawals, isolation from bank statements, or looking after money without transparency can be abuse. Elder abuse hotlines and Legal Aid can help. Attorneys must keep records—ask to see them if you are a concerned relative with standing.


## What to do next

- Agree the five household rules with someone who will hold you to them.
- Enable transaction alerts on every account holding retirement money.
- Practise calling your bank using only the number on your card.
- Bookmark Scamwatch.gov.au and myGov—type URLs yourself; do not use search ads.
- Share this page with a friend who insists "I'd never fall for that"—overconfidence is a risk too.
