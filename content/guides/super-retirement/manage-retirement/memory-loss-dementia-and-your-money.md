Memory changes make money systems fragile. Unpaid bills, duplicate donations, forgotten PINs, and vulnerability to scams often rise together—not because someone was careless, but because the brain tasks that money management requires become harder. The kindest financial step is planning **before** decisions become contested or a crisis forces rushed choices.

This is general information—not medical advice. Seek clinical assessment for new or worsening symptoms. ClearMoney does not diagnose dementia.

## Money systems and cognitive change

Early signs might include unopened mail piles, difficulty following bank statements, repeated questions about the same payment, or new secrecy about finances. Family may notice before the person does—handle conversations with dignity, not surveillance.

Money mistakes in later life are not moral failures. They are signals to simplify systems and strengthen legal safeguards while the person can still express preferences.

## Legal tools while capacity is clear

While decision-making capacity is intact, arrange enduring powers of attorney, update wills, and document preferences about gifting, aged care, and investment risk. Details: [Wills and powers of attorney](/topics/super-retirement/plan-retirement/wills-and-powers-of-attorney).

Waiting until a crisis can force tribunal applications for guardianship or administration orders—slow, stressful, and public. Solicitors who specialise in elder law can explain state differences in attorney powers and witnessing rules.

Tell your super fund and bank who holds attorney documents **before** a lockout. Certified copies beat frantic faxing during a hospital admission.

## Simplifying accounts and bills

Reduce complexity while the person can still participate:

- Consolidate to one everyday account and one savings account where possible
- Automate rent, strata, utilities, insurance, and regular donations
- Set low daily card limits; consider a separate spending account with a modest float
- Cancel unused subscriptions discovered during a statement review
- Move large balances to structures attorneys can see—without sharing passwords insecurely

Keep a simple written list of income sources, bills, and due dates on one page. Update it when something changes. Pin a copy inside a kitchen cupboard—memory-friendly locations beat filing cabinets nobody opens anymore.

Photograph the list with a phone if a family helper lives interstate; the photo is not a password, but it prevents "I didn't know the rates were due" when mail stops being opened.

## Reducing scam exposure

People with cognitive decline are prime scam targets—see [Protect your money in retirement](/topics/super-retirement/manage-retirement/protect-your-money-in-retirement). Practical steps:

- Use call-blocking on landlines and mobiles
- Remove "unknown caller" obligation to be polite—hang up is allowed
- Appoint someone to review transactions weekly for unusual transfers
- Pause unsolicited home visits from "advisers" and tradespeople

Scammers exploit shame. A weekly "anything odd this week?" chat beats interrogation.

## Banks, funds, and attorneys

Ask banks and super funds about vulnerable-customer support, third-party authorities, and financial abuse protocols. Many have specialist teams—but only if you call.

Attorneys must act in the donor's best interests and keep records. Transparent ledgers of attorney spending reduce family suspicion. Financial abuse—pressure to sign, isolation from statements, forced property transfers—requires outside help: elder abuse helplines, Legal Aid, and **1800RESPECT** if family violence is involved.

See [How to support older Australians](/topics/banking-budgeting/family/how-to-support-older-australians) if you are a family helper rather than the person experiencing change.

## Family dynamics and care costs

Siblings may disagree on spending, care, or housing. Clear roles—who pays bills, who reviews statements, who attends GP appointments—prevent duplicate effort and resentment.

Dementia journeys often move from home support to residential care. Begin My Aged Care pathways early: [Aged care](/topics/super-retirement/manage-retirement/aged-care). Care fees interact with the family home and super; planning while capacity remains improves choices later.

## Early practical swaps

Replace multiple credit cards with one low-limit card. Move to BPAY for regular merchants. Consider a joint review session each Sunday with a trusted person—fifteen minutes on balances beats a monthly shock. Remove stored card details from unfamiliar shopping sites.

## Professional assessment pathway

If you notice missed bills or confused banking, book a GP visit and ask about cognitive assessment referrals. Financial changes should follow clinical facts. Driving, cooking safety, and money safety often slip together—treat them as one plan.

## When attorneys begin to act

Start with bill-paying support while the person still participates in decisions. Abrupt total takeovers can create distress. Keep the person informed with simplified statements. If disagreements escalate, mediation and tribunal guidance exist—do not improvise large asset sales without proper authority.


## What to do next

- Complete enduring power of attorney and will updates while legal advice is still usable.
- Automate core bills and lower card limits this month—not "when things get worse."
- Nominate a transaction-review buddy and schedule a recurring calendar reminder.
- Provide certified attorney documents to your bank and super fund.
- Book a GP discussion if memory concerns are new—money systems should follow health facts, not replace them.
