Running out of money is the fear that keeps retirees awake. Spending too little—living smaller than you need while healthy—is the quieter failure. Longevity planning aims for a middle path: withdrawals you can sustain, buffers for bad markets, and permission to enjoy the years you have without betting the entire balance on one optimistic return assumption.

This guide focuses on pacing, not picking stocks. ClearMoney offers general information—not personal advice. Confirm current minimum drawdown rates and tax settings with your fund and the ATO.

## The longevity problem in plain terms

No one knows their expiry date. Account-based pensions keep paying until the balance hits zero—or until you die, whichever comes first. Age Pension helps but may not cover your preferred lifestyle. Annuities cover longevity for a slice of capital but reduce flexibility.

The maths is asymmetric: dying with money left is a estate-planning issue; outliving money is a daily survival issue. Most people overweight the first fear and underweight the second.

## Minimums versus sustainable spending

Legislation sets a **minimum** annual drawdown from account-based pensions by age. You can take more—but every extra dollar is a longevity trade. Minimums are not targets. Many retirees can sustain lower spending rates than the minimum early on, but the law still forces a minimum withdrawal.

Sketch sustainable rates with the [retirement planner](/tools/retirement-planner), then add humility for long lives, inflation, and occasional health shocks. Compare against [Work out how much you need to retire](/topics/super-retirement/plan-retirement/work-out-how-much-you-need-to-retire) from your planning phase.

Separate **essential** spending (housing, food, utilities, core health) from **discretionary** spending (travel, gifts, hobbies). Fund essentials from reliable sources first—Age Pension, annuities, predictable pension minimums—before discretionary draws float with markets.

## Surviving bad markets early on

Sequence risk hurts most in the first decade of retirement. Large market falls combined with steady withdrawals lock in losses permanently. Retirees who panic-sell growth assets after a crash often miss the recovery that follows.

Practical buffers:

- Hold one to three years of essential spending in cash or defensive assets inside or outside super
- Cut discretionary draws after a large market fall rather than selling growth units at lows
- Avoid increasing pension payments automatically after a bumper year unless the plan supports it
- Rebalance gently rather than wholesale switching after headlines

## Simple guardrails that stick

Rules you will follow beat elegant spreadsheets you abandon:

1. Essentials funded by Age Pension plus reliable income first
2. Discretionary spending adjusts with portfolio value—tighten after bad years
3. Annual review each birthday—not each news headline
4. One-off lump sums require a written purpose—see [Super lump sum](/topics/super-retirement/income-sources/super-lump-sum)
5. Keep a health and home-repair float separate from holiday money

Write the guardrails on one page and pin them where you pay bills.

## Health, inflation, and one-off costs

Prices rise. Health costs often rise faster than CPI. A payment that felt comfortable in 2022 may feel tight in 2028. Build a habit of checking whether last year's draw still buys the same life.

Health shocks need cash access without forced super sales in down markets. See [Manage health costs in retirement](/topics/super-retirement/manage-retirement/manage-health-costs-in-retirement). Large gifts to children can undermine your own floor—kindness without boundaries becomes a problem at 82.

Tax matters but should not drive unsafe spending—see [Retirement income and tax](/topics/super-retirement/manage-retirement/retirement-income-and-tax).

## Valves when the maths tightens

If projections turn sour, valves exist short of austerity:

- Return to part-time work—even one day a week resets the maths gently
- Downsize or rent a room—housing costs dominate many budgets
- Reduce discretionary categories before essentials
- Review investment fees and insurance overlaps for easy wins
- Check Age Pension eligibility if assets have fallen

Dignity belongs in the plan. Cutting every coffee is less sustainable than one structural change.

Protect balances from scams and pushy sales—see [Protect your money in retirement](/topics/super-retirement/manage-retirement/protect-your-money-in-retirement). Criminals target visible retirement assets.

## Dynamic spending rules (pick one and write it down)

**Guardrail rule:** If the portfolio falls more than a set percentage from its retirement-date value, cut discretionary spending until recovery. **Floor-and-upside rule:** Pay essentials from Age Pension plus a defensive bucket; only spend bonuses from growth years. **Age-based step-down:** Plan higher travel spending in the first decade, then lower.

The best rule is the one you will follow. Put it in the notes app you actually open.

## Bucket structures

Some retirees keep:

- Bucket A: 1–2 years cash for bills
- Bucket B: 3–5 years bonds or defensive assets
- Bucket C: long-term growth

Refill A from B or C on a schedule, not on panic days. You do not need three literal bank accounts—mental accounting plus your pension option mix can achieve the same idea.

## Longevity vs lifestyle guilt

Spending nothing so the kids get it can be a valid value choice—but name it. If the goal is your secure life with a modest bequest, reverse the default: fund your plan first, gift deliberately second. Adult children usually prefer parents who are not quietly destitute.


## What to do next

- Separate essential versus discretionary spending on one page today.
- Size a cash buffer for one to three years of essentials before the next market scare.
- Set account-based pension payments with a birthday review date in your calendar.
- Re-run the [retirement planner](/tools/retirement-planner) after any major health or housing change.
- Share your guardrails with a trusted person who will tell you the truth.
